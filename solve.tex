\section{Introduction}
This note documents a practical algorithm for projecting a chain of points with
unit-length constraints on consecutive difference vectors. The goal is not to
present a formal paper, but to capture the derivation and the key numerical
ideas behind an experimental solver. We reformulate the problem in terms of
difference vectors, derive a Newton-like system, and then exploit the resulting
block tridiagonal structure for fast direct solves. The emphasis is on clear
steps and implementation-friendly formulas rather than theoretical optimality
guarantees.
\section{Formulation}
We're going to solve
\begin{align}
\min_{\vv x} \quad &  \frac{1}{2} \; \vv{(x-p)}^{\T} \vv M \vv {(x-p)}
\label{eq:orig} \\
\text{s.t} \quad & \vv z = \vv R \vv x \quad \mathrm{ and} \quad \vv z_i^{\T}\vv
z_i = 1 \nonumber
\end{align}
where
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabular}{@{}cclp{0.6\textwidth}@{}}
$n$ & $\in$ & $\mathbb N$ & is the number of particles \\
$m$ & $\in$ & $\mathbb N$ & is the number of links, $m = n-1$ \\
$\vv x$ & $\in$ & $\mathbb R^{3n}$ & is the solution vector, \\
$\vv p$ & $\in$ & $\mathbb R^{3n}$ & is target/free moving positions, \\
$\vv z$ & $\in$ & $\mathbb R^{\mathrm{3m}}$ & is the vector of difference vectors in the chain, \\
$\vv z_i$ & $\in$ & $\mathbb R^{\mathrm{3}}$ & is the $i$th 3-vector in $\vv z$, \\
$\vv R$ & $\in$  & $\mathbb R^{\mathrm{3m\times3n}}$ & is the per-edge
finite-difference matrix so that $(\vv R \vv x)_i = \vv x_{i} - \vv x_{i+1}$ \\
$\vv M$ & $\in$ & $\mathbb R^{\mathrm{3n\times3n}}$ & is the mass matrix, simply
having the mass (or weight) of each particle in each $3\times3$ block diagonal \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
We want to be working only on the difference vectors in $\vv z$. To do that, we
need to eliminate $\vv x$, and we do this by holding $\vv z$ constant and
deriving the optimality conditions for \eqref{eq:orig}. We first state the
Lagrangian:
\begin{align*}
\mathcal{L}(\vv x, \vv \lambda) = \frac{1}{2} \vv{(x-p)}^{\T} \vv M \vv{(x-p)} +
\vv \lambda^{\T} (\vv z - \vv R \vv x)
\end{align*}
with the optimality conditions:
\begin{align*}
 \vv M (\vv x - \vv p) - \vv R^{\T} \vv \lambda &= 0 \\
 \vv z - \vv R \vv x &= 0
\end{align*}
solving for $\vv \lambda$ gives us:
\begin{align*}
\vv \lambda = (\underbrace{\vv R \vv M^{-1} \vv R^{\T}}_{S})^{-1}
(\vv z-\vv{Rp})
\end{align*}
where $\vv S = \vv R \vv M^{-1} \vv R^{\T} \in \mathbb R^{3m \times 3m}$ is a
block tridiagonal SPD matrix. We use this to give us an expression for $\vv x$:
\begin{align*}
\vv x = \vv p + \vv M^{-1}\vv R^{\T} \vv S^{-1}(\vv z - \vv R\vv p)
\end{align*}
Inserting this into \eqref{eq:orig} eliminates $\mathbf z = \mathbf R \mathbf x
$ and we are left with
\begin{align}
\min_{\vv z} \quad &  \frac{1}{2} \; \vv{(z-Rp)}^{\T} \vv S^{-1} \vv
{(z-Rp)} \label{eq:main}\\
\text{s.t} \quad & \vv z_i^{\T}\vv z_i = 1 \quad i=1\dots m \nonumber
\end{align}
In this reformulated problem we must find a $\vv z$ that consists of unit length
3-vectors that minimize the distance to $\vv{Rp}$ under the $\vv S^{-1}$-norm.
Note that it is trivial to project $\vv z$ to satisfy the unit length
constraints (under the regular $\vv I$-norm, known as normalization), and
likewise it is trivial to
obtain $\vv x$ given any $\vv z$.
This means that whenever we take a Newton step on $\vv z$ we can trivially rein
it to its feasible solution, and in effect only change the directions of
each $\vv z_i$ vector, never their lengths. Throughout, $\vv M$, $\vv R$,
$\vv S$, and $\vv p$ are fixed inputs; $\vv z$ is the variable we iterate, and
$\vv \lambda$ is re-estimated each loop.
\section{Solution}
We want to solve this system using a Newton-like method, so to make sense of
that we first derive the full Newton step. The Lagrangian of \eqref{eq:main} is
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}_{(\vv z, \vv \lambda)} = \frac{1}{2} \vv{(z-Rp)}^{\T} \vv S^{-1}
\vv{(z-Rp)} + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \lambda_i (\vv z_i^{\T} \vv z_i - 1)
\label{eq:lagrange}
\end{align}
The optimality conditions for \eqref{eq:lagrange} are:
\begin{align*}
\vv S^{-1} (\vv z - \vv{Rp}) + \vv{Q_z}^{\T} \vv \lambda &= 0 \\
\vv z_i^{\T} \vv z_i - 1 &= 0 \quad i=1,\dots,m
\end{align*}
and the Newton step to find a stationary point $\vv{(z,\lambda)}$ for
\eqref{eq:lagrange} is
\begin{align}
\underbrace{
\begin{bmatrix}
\vv S^{-1}+\vv{D_\lambda} & \vv Q^{\T}_z \\
\vv Q_z & \vv 0 \\
\end{bmatrix}
}_{H \mathcal{L}_{(\vv z, \vv \lambda)}   }
\begin{bmatrix}
\Delta \vv z \\
\Delta \vv \lambda \\
\end{bmatrix}
=
\,-\underbrace{
\begin{bmatrix}
\vv S^{-1}(\vv z - \vv R \vv p) + \vv Q_z^{\T} \vv \lambda \\
\frac{1}{2}\vv{(Q_z z-1)} \\
\end{bmatrix}
}_{ \nabla \mathcal{L}_{(\vv z, \vv \lambda)}  } \label{eq:newton}
\end{align}
where
\begin{align*}
\vv Q_z=
\begin{bmatrix}
\vv z_1^{\T} & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
0 & \vv z_2^{\T} & \cdots & 0 \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & \cdots & \vv z_m^{\T}
\end{bmatrix}  \in \mathbb R^{ m\times 3m}
\; \vv D_\lambda  =
\begin{bmatrix}
\vv{I_{\lambda_1}} & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
0 & \vv{I_{\lambda_2}} & \cdots & 0 \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & \cdots & \vv{I_{\lambda_m}}
\end{bmatrix}
 \in \mathbb R^{3m\times 3m} \\
\end{align*}
There are many interesting ways to go about solving this system, but in our case
the goal is to exploit the tridiagonal structure of $\vv S$, in order to
use fast direct solves. Instead of solving the full step, we first solve just
for $\vv{\Delta \lambda}$ at $\vv{\lambda = 0}$, effectively resetting the
multipliers in each iteration. This is not standard Newton, but in practice it
improves stability and keeps each linear system sparse and well-conditioned.
After that we solve for $\vv \Delta z$ to update the solution variables.

\subsection{Solving for $\vv{\lambda}$}
To derive the solution for \(\Delta \lambda\) we eliminate \(\Delta z\) by using
$\vv{\lambda = 0}$ and solving the first row in \eqref{eq:newton}.
Interestingly, because we will
project the vectors in $\vv z$ in each iteration, the $\vv{Q_z z-1}$ term is
always $0$, which will simplify things a bit:
\begin{align*}
\Delta \vv z = -\vv S (\vv Q^{\T}_z \Delta \vv \lambda + \vv S^{-1}(\vv z - \vv R
\vv p))
\end{align*}
Substitute \(\Delta \vv z\) into the second row in \eqref{eq:newton}:
\begin{align*}
\vv Q_z (-\vv S (\vv Q^{\T}_z \Delta \vv \lambda + \vv S^{-1}(\vv z - \vv R \vv
p))) = \frac{1}{2}\underbrace{\vv{(Q_z z-1)}}_{\vv 0}
\end{align*}
Simplifying to solve for \(\Delta \vv \lambda\), we get:
\[
\vv Q_z \vv S \vv Q^{\T}_z \Delta \vv \lambda = -\vv Q_z (\vv z - \vv R \vv p)
\]
This system is tridiagonal because the operator \(\vv{ Q_z(\cdot)Q_z^{\T}} \)
collapses each \( 3 \times 3 \) block in \( \vv S \) to a scalar
value \(\vv{ z_i^T S_{(i,j)} z_j }\). This operation effectively reduces the
block
tridiagonal structure of \(\vv S \) to the plain tridiagonal structure in \(\vv{
Q_zSQ_z^{\T}} \in \mathbb R^{m\times m} \). Also note that $\vv Q_z z = 1$
appears in the RHS and so it could be further reduced to $\vv{Q_z R p - 1}$ but
since the vector $\vv{z-Rp}$ is needed elsewhere this has negligible practical
benefit.

\subsection{Solving for $\vv{\Delta z}$}
To solve for \(\Delta \vv z\), we substitute \(\Delta \lambda = 0\) into the
first row of \eqref{eq:newton}:
\begin{align}
\vv{(S^{-1} + D_\lambda) \Delta z = -S^{-1} (z - Rp) - Q_z^{\T} \lambda}
\label{eq:solve_z}
\end{align}
To avoid having to solve the dense system $\vv{S^{-1}+D_\lambda}$ we exploit
that we can cheaply compute the cholesky decomposition $\vv{ LL^{\T} = S}$ and
make use of the identity
\begin{align}
\vv{S^{-1} + D_\lambda = L^{-T} (I + L^T D_\lambda L) L^{-1}} \label{eq:ident}
\end{align}
We insert \eqref{eq:ident} into \eqref{eq:solve_z}

\begin{align*}
\vv{L^{-T} (I + L^T D_\lambda L) L^{-1} \Delta z} &= \vv{-L^{-T} L^{-1} (z - Rp) - Q_z^{\T} \lambda} \\
\end{align*}
Then, multiplying with the invertible $\vv L^{\T}$
\begin{align*}
\vv{(I + L^T D_\lambda L)} \vv{L^{-1} \Delta z} &= \vv{-L^{-1} (z - Rp) - L^T
Q_z^{\T} \lambda}
\end{align*}
means we can solve for $\vv{u=L^{-1}}\Delta z$ and then obtain $\vv \Delta z$
like so
\begin{align*}
\vv{(I + L^T D_\lambda L) u} &=  \vv{-L^{-1} (z - Rp) - L^T Q_z^{\T} \lambda}
\end{align*}
and then finally
\begin{align*}
\Delta \vv{z} &= \vv{L u}
\end{align*}
The great advantage here is that $\vv{(I + L^T D_\lambda L) }$ remains block
tridiagonal and so it's fast to solve. The disadvantage is that we need one
extra
solve of the block bidiagonal $\vv L$ to compute $\vv{L^{-1} (z - Rp)}$. This is
annoying but still greatly outweighs the pain of solving a dense system using
iterative methods. Note also that in the trivial case where $\vv{\lambda = 0}$
the system reduces to the identity. In an effort to keep the eigenvalues of
$\vv{(I + L^T D_\lambda L) }$
positive, we clamp $\vv \lambda$ to positive values before we compute $\vv
D_\lambda$. This is a hack but seems to work well in practice. We now proceed to
list the full algorithm.

\section{Algorithm}
Here we'll outline the algorithm in full before explaining each step in detail:
\begin{center}
\begin{lstlisting}[]
$\vv L$ = cholesky($\vv{S}$)
$\vv z$ = $\vv{Rp}$  // or warm start from previous $\vv z$
while true do
    $\vv Q$,$\vv z$ = normalize($\vv z$)
    $\vv y$ = $\vv{Rp-z}$
    $\vv \lambda$ = solve($\vv{QSQ^{\T}}$, $\vv{Qy}$)
    $\vv{b_z}$ = solve($\vv L$, $\vv{y}$)
    $\vv{b_\lambda}$ = $\vv{L^{\T}Q^{\T}\lambda} $
    if $\frac{\|\vv{b_z-b_\lambda}\|}{\|\vv{R p}\|}<\epsilon_{tol}$ then
        $\vv s$ = solve($\vv{L^{\T}}$, $\vv{b_z}$)
        $\vv x$ = $\vv{p-M^{-1}R^{\T}s}$
        return $\vv x$
    $\vv D$ = diagonal(max($0$,$\vv \lambda$))
    $\vv{\Delta z}$ = $\vv L$ solve($\vv{I+L^{\T}DL}$, $\vv{b_z-b_\lambda}$)
    $\vv z$ = $\vv{z+\Delta z}$
\end{lstlisting}
\end{center}
Each iteration the algorithm does these main steps:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Normalize the 3-vectors in $\vv z$
    \item Compute the $n-1$ Lagrange multipliers of the system at $\vv z$
    \item Check the solution residual, if below threshold compute and return $\vv x$
    \item Compute the Newton step on $\vv z$ and go to step 1
\end{enumerate}
Note the following:
\begin{itemize}
    \item we precompute the Cholesky factor $\vv{LL^{\T}=S}$ once if $\vv M$ is
    fixed, and reuse it in every iteration to keep all solves sparse
    \item the sign convention is handled by setting $\vv y = \vv{Rp-z}$, so the
    Newton right-hand side becomes $\vv{b_z-b_\lambda}$ in the factored system
    \item $\vv Q$ is block diagonal with row blocks $\vv z_i^{\T}$, so
    $\vv{QSQ^{\T}}$ is plain tridiagonal and fast to solve
    \item clamping $\vv \lambda$ before forming $\vv D$ keeps
    $\vv{I+L^{\T}DL}$ positive definite; this is a pragmatic stabilizer rather
    than a theoretical guarantee
\end{itemize}
\section{Test}
We find that the best way to get a feel for how this algorithm behaves in
animation and/or simulation settings, is to play around with the interactive
demo. The C implementation used in the following tests as well as in the demo is
a naive
implementation that just operates on the raw input. There is no scaling,
clamping, or other techniques to avoid blow-ups or errors. Notably there is no
line-search type handling of the step-size, the solution is simply
normalized per $\vv{z_i}$ vector after each update. As is probably expected, the
algorithm converges
more slowly when the difference between the initial starting solution and the
final solution is large. Often the initial solution is close to the converged
solution in animation problem. Still the algorithm shows decent linear
convergence to machine precision in the following tests, where the final
solution if far from the initial and also far from the target positions. We find
that in animation type scenarios, convergence can be much faster.
